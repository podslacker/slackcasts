The video breaks down the hill‑country blues tune “Goin’ Down South” (made famous by R.L. Burnside and the Black Keys). Topics Covered

  • Hill‑Country vs. Delta blues – Emphasizes the short, repetitive riffs and minimal chord changes that make the genre ideal for hand‑exercise.
  • Structure of the main riff – First four bars (three bars of 4‑beat plus a 6‑beat fourth bar) and the “second riff” that repeats three times before returning to the first.
  • Detailed tab walk‑through – Shows each note (5th‑fret D string, open E, 2nd‑fret D, etc.), tiny quarter‑step bends, and the timing of the fifth‑fret double‑note on the fourth bar.
  • Right‑hand technique #1: Two‑finger pop – Uses thumb + index to pluck low strings and pops the high‑E/B strings with the middle and ring fingers; emphasizes short, percussive note stops with the left hand.
  • Right‑hand technique #2: Back‑hand strum + slap – Rotational wrist motion where the back of the hand strums the top two strings while the thumb “slaps” the low string, adding extra groove.
  • Practice suggestions – Learn the riff first with the basic pop approach, then try the back‑hand strum, and finally combine both; use a slow metronome to lock in timing.
  • Performance tip – Keep the slap light; focus on clean note timing and the percussive feel of the back‑hand stroke.

Key Takeaways

  • Hill‑country blues relies on tight, repeating riffs; mastering one riff often means you can play the whole song.
  • The main riff consists of two parts: a four‑bar groove (with a 6‑beat final bar) and a three‑bar “second riff” that leads back.
  • Tiny quarter‑step bends give the essential blues flavor—don’t over‑bend.
  • Two right‑hand options: conventional two‑finger pop (easier to start) or a back‑hand strum with thumb slap (adds groove and is useful across genres).
  • Proper left‑hand muting after each pop creates the short, snappy sound characteristic of the style.
  • Practice slowly with a metronome, then increase tempo once the motions feel comfortable.
