## Overview

## Topics Covered  

- **Hill‑country blues vs. Delta blues** – Emphasis on repetitive, groove‑based riffs with minimal chord changes.  
- **Structure of the riff** – First four bars (8‑beat pattern) followed by a second four‑bar variation; the fourth measure extends to six beats.  
- **Finger placement & note detail** – Main notes on the D string (5th‑2nd‑5th‑2nd) with a tiny quarter‑step bend, plus open low E and higher‑string pops.  
- **First right‑hand approach** – Simultaneous thumb‑index pluck on low strings and pop of the high E/B strings; importance of short, crisp pops and using the left hand to mute immediately.  
- **Second right‑hand approach** – Back‑hand (downward) strum on the top two strings combined with a thumb slap on the low E for added percussion; rotational wrist motion rather than arm swing.  
- **Practice workflow** – Learn the tab with the basic pop technique first, then add the back‑hand strum, eventually combining both.  
- **Slow metronome play‑through** – Demonstrates all eight bars in both right‑hand styles for learners to play along.  
- **Performance tips** – Keep the slap light, focus on clean note placement, and maintain a relaxed rotational wrist motion for the back‑hand technique.

## Key Takeaways  

- Hill‑country blues relies on tight, repeating riffs; mastering one groove unlocks the whole song.  
- The riff consists of a 5th‑2nd‑5th‑2nd pattern on the D string with a small bend, plus coordinated pops on the high strings.  
- Two viable right‑hand methods: simple two‑finger pops or a back‑hand strum plus thumb slap for extra groove.  
- Use the left hand to mute popped notes immediately for a crisp, percussive sound.  
- Practice slowly with a metronome, then layer the back‑hand technique once the basic pattern is solid.  
- The back‑hand motion is a wrist‑rotation, not an arm movement; keep it light to retain tone.
