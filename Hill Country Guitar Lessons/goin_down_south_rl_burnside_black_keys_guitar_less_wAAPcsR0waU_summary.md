## Overview  
In this lesson the instructor breaks down the hill‑country blues tune **“Goin’ Down South”** (made famous by R.L. Burnside and covered by The Black Keys). He explains the style’s riff‑based, groove‑focused nature, then teaches the main eight‑bar riff in two parts and demonstrates two different right‑hand techniques for playing it. The video ends with a slow metronome play‑through of both approaches so viewers can practice along.

## Topics Covered
- **Hill‑Country vs. Delta Blues** – Emphasis on repetitive riffs and minimal chord changes, making it ideal for hand‑exercise and groove practice.  
- **Structure of the Riff** – First four bars (8‑beat pattern: three bars of 4 beats, fourth bar of 6 beats) and the second four‑bar variation that repeats three times before returning to the first riff.  
- **Detailed Fingerings** – Uses thumb + index on low E and D strings, with “pop” of the high E and B strings; includes a tiny quarter‑step bend on the D‑string fifth fret.  
- **Right‑Hand Approach #1 (Standard Pop)** – Separate fingers pop the top strings; keep the pops short and mute them with the left hand for a crisp sound.  
- **Right‑Hand Approach #2 (Backhand Strum + Slap)** – A rotational “backhand” strum hits the top two strings while the thumb slaps the low string, adding percussive groove.  
- **Practice Tips** – Start with the standard pop technique, then add the backhand strum; focus on wrist rotation rather than arm movement; keep the slap light.  
- **Full Play‑Through** – Slow metronome run‑through of all eight bars in both styles for audience to jam along.

## Key Takeaways
- Hill‑country blues relies on short, looping riffs rather than chord changes, perfect for developing right‑hand groove.  
- The main riff consists of a repeating 4‑beat pattern with a 6‑beat fourth measure; mastering this loop covers most of the song.  
- Small bends and muted “pops” give the riff its characteristic blues feel.  
- Two viable right‑hand techniques: traditional two‑finger pop and a backhand strum/slap combo; the latter adds extra percussive texture.  
- Practice slowly with a metronome, first mastering finger placement, then integrating the backhand motion.  
- Wrist rotation (like turning a key) is the key motion for the backhand strum—don’t rely on arm movement.  

## Notable Quotes
- “Hill country blues is really more riff‑based or groove‑based – they won’t change the chords as much.”  
- “The tiny quarter‑step bend is just to give it that blues bend, not a full bend.”  
- “When you add the backhand strum you get a little more rhythm, a little more groove – that’s the whole concept for hill‑country style.”  
- “Your wrist stays put; it’s a rotational motion, like opening a key at a lock—not an arm swing.”
